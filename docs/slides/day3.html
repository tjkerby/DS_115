Day 3: Data Science in the Workplace

What do employers mean by “data science,” and how can I learn from people doing it?

DS 115: Exploring Data Science

Today’s investigation

Data science is broad. Today we test that claim using real job postings.

What we will do today

1. Read job postings

Look past the title and examine the actual work.

2. Compare roles

Notice where titles help and where they mislead.

3. Learn from people

Use informational interviews as career research.

A job title is a clue. The responsibilities are stronger evidence.

Common titles, rough tendencies

Data Analyst

Understand data, build reports, answer business questions, communicate findings.

Data Scientist

Modeling, statistics, prediction, experimentation, and decision support.

Data Engineer

Build reliable pipelines, warehouses, infrastructure, and trusted data systems.

Analytics Engineer

Transform raw data into governed, reusable, analysis-ready data models.

ML Engineer

Build machine-learning systems and put models into products.

MLOps Engineer

Deploy, monitor, retrain, govern, and maintain machine-learning systems.

Statistician / Biostatistician

Study design, inference, uncertainty, and statistical methodology.

AI/ML Engineer

Build AI and ML applications, often using LLMs, cloud tools, and production systems.

A warning before we start

These are tendencies, not definitions.

Which two titles do you think will be hardest to distinguish from a job posting alone?

How to read each posting

1. Work

What would this person spend time doing?

2. Skills

What tools, methods, and habits show up?

3. Center of gravity

Is this mostly analysis, modeling, infrastructure, deployment, research, consulting, or communication?

4. Guess

What title would you give this role if the employer hid it?

Same title, different jobs

Employers can use the same title for jobs with very different centers of gravity.

What they still have in common

Data

They all turn messy information into usable evidence.

Computation

They all require programming and technical fluency.

Quantitative reasoning

They all use models, statistics, or evaluation.

Communication

They all need results to affect decisions or products.

Job postings are useful, but incomplete

A posting tells you what an organization says it wants. It does not fully tell you what the work feels like.

What do we still not know?

  • What does a normal week look like?
  • Which skills get used every day?
  • Which requirements are flexible?
  • What is surprisingly hard?
  • What is surprisingly enjoyable?
  • How did this person get started?
  • What helped them most in college?
  • What would they do differently?
  • What adjacent roles should I know about?
  • What skills are underrated?

If the posting cannot answer those questions, someone doing the work often can.

Informational interviews

An informational interview is a short conversation with someone doing work you want to understand.

The goal

Learn from someone with lived experience in a role, field, company, or career path.

This is career research, not asking for a job.

Who should you talk to?

Look for someone who:

  • Works in or near an area you are curious about
  • Is perhaps 1 to 7 years into their career
  • Has a path you can imagine yourself following
  • Might remember the student-to-career transition
  • Does not need to have the exact title you think you want

A person doing interesting work with an unfamiliar title may be more useful than someone with the exact title you expected.

Where can you find people?

Warm connections

Friends, family, professors, TAs, classmates, club leaders.

Shared communities

BYU alumni, student groups, local companies, professional events.

Thoughtful outreach

LinkedIn, company pages, conference speakers, people whose work you encounter online.

Warm connection is easiest. Shared connection is next. Thoughtful cold outreach can still work.

Which message is easier to say yes to?

Message A

Hi, I am interested in data science. Can I pick your brain about your career?

Message B

Hi Maya, I am a BYU student exploring careers in data science. I saw that you work in product analytics at Acme, and I am trying to better understand what that kind of work looks like. Would you be willing to talk for about 20 minutes sometime in the next two weeks? I would especially love to hear how you moved from school into your current role.

Why is Message B easier to answer?

How informal mentoring often begins

When someone gives genuinely good advice, follow it.

Then tell them what happened.

Listen, act, report back

Listen

Take the conversation seriously. Write down specific advice.

Act

Try one thing they recommended: a course, project, tool, club, book, or conversation.

Report back

Thank them again and share what you did and what you learned.

You demonstrate that you listened, acted, and that their advice had an impact. That is often how an informal mentoring relationship can begin.

A few etiquette rules

Do

  • Show up prepared
  • Respect the agreed time
  • Ask follow-up questions
  • Listen more than you talk
  • Send a thank-you

Avoid

  • Asking them to get you a job
  • Sending a resume unless relevant or requested
  • Monopolizing their time
  • Treating the conversation as transactional

Before you leave today

  1. Pick one area you want to investigate.
  2. Find three possible people to contact.
  3. Choose the person you would most like to contact first.
  4. Draft a message using the outreach formula.
  5. Have a neighbor read it and give feedback.

For the assignment, thoughtful outreach attempts matter. You should not be penalized if no one responds.

Day 3 takeaway

Do not choose a career based only on a title. Investigate the work.

Job postings are one source of evidence. People doing the work are another.
